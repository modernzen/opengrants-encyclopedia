# How to Search for Grants

Effective grant search starts with hard filters — eligibility, geography, and funder type — before topic keywords. Search the funder and the awards already made, not only the open opportunity list. Then convert every productive search into a standing subscription, so new opportunities arrive without a hunt.

## Key takeaways

- Filter on eligibility and geography before you filter on topic.
- Funders name programs in their vocabulary, not yours.
- Awarded-project databases reveal fit that opportunity listings hide.
- Saved searches with subscriptions beat episodic hunting.
- Free federal sources are excellent; free foundation sources lag by years.

## Why does keyword search fail when you search for grants?

Keyword search fails because funders describe programs in their own institutional vocabulary, not in the words an applicant would use. A youth employment program may be indexed as career pathways, opportunity youth, workforce innovation, or by a statutory program name that contains none of those words. The words you type are the words you already know.

Search mechanics make the mismatch worse. On Grants.gov, the default behavior of the keyword box is a union, not an intersection: the Search Tips page states that OR "is the default conjunction operator. If there is no operator between two words, the operator is used" ([Grants.gov, Search Tips](https://www.grants.gov/search-tips)). Typing *rural broadband adoption* therefore returns everything containing *rural*, *broadband*, or *adoption* — thousands of unrelated records that look like a thin market.

The corrective is to treat keywords as the last filter rather than the first. Eligibility and geography are binary and knowable; topic is fuzzy and negotiable. A grant search that begins with entity type and place produces a small, real set. A grant search that begins with a subject phrase produces a large, mostly ineligible set that takes hours to disqualify.

Vocabulary can also be recovered rather than guessed. Pull three or four awards that resemble your project from a funded-award database, read the abstracts, and harvest the terms the agency actually used. Those terms — not yours — are the ones that match the indexed text.

## Which filters should you apply first in a grant search?

Apply the disqualifying filters first. In a grant search, eligibility, geography, funder type, and opportunity status eliminate most of the field in seconds and never require judgment. Topic, award size, and deadline fit are second-order screens applied to whatever survives.

There are six filters worth applying in order before any keyword is typed:

- **Applicant entity type.** Nonprofit, government, tribal entity, institution of higher education, small business, or individual. Most federal opportunities restrict this absolutely, and the restriction is stated in the eligibility section of the announcement. See [grant eligibility explained](/encyclopedia/readiness/grant-eligibility-explained/) for how these categories are constructed.
- **Geography.** Place of performance, service area, or state of incorporation. Foundation geography is often narrower than a foundation's own website suggests.
- **Funder type.** Federal, state, local, foundation, corporate. Each has a different discovery system, and no single search covers all of them.
- **Opportunity status.** Forecasted, posted, closed, or archived. Closed and archived records are research material, not targets.
- **Registration prerequisites.** Federal applications require an active entity registration, which is not instantaneous; see [SAM.gov registration and the UEI](/encyclopedia/readiness/sam-gov-and-uei-registration/).
- **Cost share.** A required non-federal match you cannot document is a disqualifier, not a detail.

Only after those six does a topic keyword do useful work. Applied in the other order, a search returns opportunities you are structurally barred from, and the time spent reading them is unrecoverable.

## How do you search Grants.gov precisely?

Grants.gov is the government-wide find-and-apply portal for federal discretionary funding, and its search offers three identifier fields — keyword, opportunity number, and Assistance Listings — alongside filters for opportunity status, funding instrument type, eligibility, category, and agency ([Grants.gov, Search Grants](https://www.grants.gov/search-grants)). The Department of Education states the portal's role plainly: "The definitive list of U.S. federal government grants is located at Grants.gov" ([U.S. Department of Education, Available Grants](https://www2.ed.gov/fund/grant/find/edlite-forecast.html)).

Precision comes from the operators. The Grants.gov keyword box supports quoted exact phrases, AND, NOT, required-term (`+`) and excluded-term (`-`) prefixes, single- and multi-character wildcards, proximity search, and fuzzy matching ([Grants.gov, Search Tips](https://www.grants.gov/search-tips)). A query such as `"water conservation" AND "habitat restoration"` behaves as an intersection; the same words unquoted and unjoined behave as a union.

Two identifiers do more work than any phrase. The **funding opportunity number** is the announcement's unique handle and is the only reliable way to return to a specific competition. The **Assistance Listing Number**, formerly the CFDA number, is a five-digit `XX.XXX` code identifying the federal program itself, where the first two digits identify the administering agency ([Grants.gov, Grant Terminology](https://www.grants.gov/learn-grants/grant-terminology)). Searching by Assistance Listing Number finds every competition run under a program across years, including the closed ones.

Forecast records are the other underused surface. A forecasted record "alerts applicants to a funding opportunity that is planned but that has not yet an official, posted Funding Opportunity Announcement," and it carries the opportunity number and eligibility information — though Grants.gov warns "it is not guaranteed that a grant forecast will become an FOA" ([Grants.gov, Forecast Tab](https://apply07.grants.gov/help/html/help/SearchGrants/ForecastTab.htm)). Forecasts are how you buy planning time, because the posted window itself is short by regulation; see the current figures above.

## How do you search for the funder instead of the opportunity?

Searching the funder means starting from money already awarded rather than from money being advertised. Every federal grant award is public, and the award record tells you what an agency actually buys — which organizations, at what size, in what places — rather than what a program description says it hopes to buy.

Three federal award databases carry the analysis:

- **USAspending.gov** — the official open data source for federal awards, with an advanced search that filters by Assistance Listing, recipient, recipient type, location, agency, award type, award description, and amount ([USAspending, Advanced Search](https://www.usaspending.gov/search)). Filtering by Assistance Listing plus your state answers the question no announcement answers: has this program ever funded an organization like mine, here?
- **NIH RePORTER** — project-level search across NIH-funded research, including a Matchmaker function that identifies "potential Program Officials, ICs, and review panels" from your own abstract text ([NIH RePORTER](https://reporter.nih.gov/)).
- **SAM.gov Assistance Listings** — "detailed public descriptions of federal programs that provide grants, loans, scholarships, insurance, and other types of assistance awards," browsable across all agencies ([SAM.gov, Assistance Listings](https://sam.gov/content/assistance-listings)).

The workflow that produces the best return is backward. Identify three organizations doing work adjacent to yours, look up their federal awards by recipient name, and read the Assistance Listing Numbers attached. Those listing numbers are your search terms. Run each one forward through the opportunity portal to find the live competition, and read the program page on the agency's own site, which usually carries prior-year abstracts, program officer contacts, and application archives that the announcement omits. Reading the resulting announcement is a separate discipline covered in [how to read a NOFO](/encyclopedia/finding-funding/how-to-read-a-nofo/).

## Where do you search for state, local, and foundation grants?

Grants.gov does not list state, local, or private funding, so those require separate systems. State discovery increasingly runs through statutory portals: California's Grants Portal exists because the Grant Information Act of 2018 required the State Library to build one website "that provides a centralized location … to find state grant opportunities," and a later amendment required agencies to submit post-award data on each awardee ([California Grants Portal, About This Site](https://www.grants.ca.gov/about/)). Not every state has one, and where none exists, the granting agency's own program page is the primary record.

Foundation search runs on tax filings. Private foundations file Form 990-PF, which itemizes every grant paid, and public charities file Form 990 with Schedule I listing grants above a reporting threshold ([IRS, Schedule I (Form 990)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f990si.pdf)). Two free tools sit on top of that data:

- **ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer** — free access to filed 990 and 990-PF documents with organization search, people search, and full-text search across filings ([ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/)).
- **IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search** — the authoritative database, covering Publication 78 eligibility data, the auto-revocation list, determination letters, and copies of returns ([IRS, Tax Exempt Organization Search](https://apps.irs.gov/app/eos/)).

The honest boundary between free and paid sits at aggregation and coding. Free sources give you documents; paid databases give you normalized, subject-coded, cross-funder search. Both share the same lag — annual return data takes six months to two years to reach an aggregator, per the current figures above. Commercial aggregators, including [OpenGrants](https://www.opengrants.io/), are a discovery layer over these primary records, not a replacement for reading them. The forensic method for reading a foundation's own return works on any funder, at no cost.

## How do you set up recurring grant monitoring?

Recurring monitoring replaces the search session with a subscription. Grants.gov offers a nightly opportunity update email with three independent options: **all new opportunities**, **saved searches**, and **selected opportunities** you have chosen to follow ([Grants.gov, Manage Subscriptions](https://grants.gov/connect/manage-subscriptions/)). Each answers a different question, and most organizations should run all three.

Saved searches are the workhorse. Any search built on the Search Grants page can be saved with a name and subscribed to, then edited later to widen or narrow it ([Grants.gov, Subscribe to Saved Searches](https://www.grants.gov/help/html/help/Connect/SubscribeToSavedSearches.htm)). Build one saved search per eligibility-plus-geography combination rather than one per topic; the topic terms drift, the structural filters do not.

Two limits are worth knowing before relying on subscriptions. Saved-search subscriptions notify you about matching opportunities, not about changes to an opportunity package such as modified application forms — that requires subscribing to the specific opportunity ([Grants.gov, Manage Subscriptions](https://grants.gov/connect/manage-subscriptions/)). And no subscription covers agency forecast pages that are published outside the portal, which is why the agency program page belongs on a quarterly manual check.

Monitoring cadence should follow the funder type. Federal opportunity subscriptions are daily and automatic. State portals warrant a monthly sweep. Foundation research is annual, because the underlying filings are annual, and rechecking a 990 more often than the filing cycle produces no new information. Sequencing those checks against your deadlines is the subject of [grant timing and the funding calendar](/encyclopedia/finding-funding/grant-calendar-and-timing/).

## What goes wrong in a grant search?

Grant search fails in patterned ways, and the patterns are structural rather than technical. Six recur often enough to design against:

- **Searching topic first.** Produces a large set that is mostly ineligible, and burns the hours that qualification needs.
- **Trusting the portal's default operator.** An unquoted multi-word query on Grants.gov is a union, so the result count is meaningless as a signal of opportunity volume.
- **Reading only open opportunities.** Closed and archived records carry the review criteria, award sizes, and prior grantee profile that predict whether the next round is winnable.
- **Believing a foundation's stated geography.** The funded grant list in the tax filing is evidence; the website's "national" language often is not.
- **Ignoring forecast records.** The posted window can be as short as the regulatory floor, which is not enough time to build a competitive federal application from scratch.
- **Treating search as an event.** An organization that searches when it needs money searches under time pressure, which is the condition under which bad fits look acceptable.

The last failure is the expensive one, because it feeds directly into pursuit decisions. Search volume is not the goal; a qualified, dated, and staged set of prospects is. That set is what [building a grant pipeline](/encyclopedia/finding-funding/building-a-grant-pipeline/) constructs.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Grants.gov the only place to find federal grants?

Grants.gov is the government-wide portal and the Department of Education calls it "the definitive list of U.S. federal government grants," but individual agencies also publish forecasts and program pages outside it. Agency program pages usually carry prior-year abstracts, contacts, and application archives that the portal record omits.

### What is an Assistance Listing Number?

An Assistance Listing Number, formerly the CFDA number, is a five-digit `XX.XXX` identifier for a federal assistance program, where the first two digits identify the administering agency. Searching by Assistance Listing Number returns every competition run under that program, including closed ones, which is how you study a program's history.

### How do I find grants for an individual rather than an organization?

Most federal assistance flows to organizations and to defined beneficiary classes under authorizing statutes rather than to individuals directly. Where individual eligibility exists, it appears explicitly in the eligibility section of the announcement. Filtering on applicant entity type before anything else surfaces those cases quickly.

### Can I search foundation grants without paying for a database?

Yes. Private foundations itemize every grant paid on Form 990-PF, and those filings are free through ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer and the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search. Paid databases add normalization, subject coding, and cross-funder search speed, not access to otherwise-secret data.

### How current is foundation grant data?

Annual return data takes six months to two years to reach aggregated grants databases from the date of filing, and sector-wide data for a given year tends to be complete about two years after that year ends. Every foundation profile built from tax filings is therefore a dated photograph and should be checked against the funder's own site.

### What is a forecasted opportunity?

A forecast is an agency's advance notice of a funding opportunity it plans to publish, carrying the opportunity number and eligibility information before the official announcement exists. Forecasts are planning instruments; agencies state that a forecast is not guaranteed to become a posted announcement.
