# How to Read a NOFO

Read a Notice of Funding Opportunity backward. Start with the review criteria and their weights, then the application content and format rules, then eligibility, and only then the program narrative. Build a requirements checklist line by line as you read, and allocate pages in proportion to points.

## Key takeaways

- Read the review criteria before the program description.
- Scoring weights should govern how you allocate pages.
- Responsiveness rules disqualify applications before any scoring.
- Registration deadlines precede submission deadlines by weeks.
- Program policy factors let a top-scored application lose legitimately.

## What sections does a federal NOFO contain?

A federal Notice of Funding Opportunity follows a required template set in Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200, which specifies eight numbered sections covering basic information, eligibility, program description, application contents and format, submission requirements and deadlines, application review information, award notices, and post-award administration ([Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/appendix-Appendix%20I%20to%20Part%20200)).

Two numbering conventions circulate. Older announcements — and many agency templates built on them — use letters A through H, where Section A is the program description, Section C is eligibility, Section D is application and submission information, Section E is application review information, and Section H is other information. The numbered scheme rearranges the order but preserves the content. Applicants encounter both, sometimes in the same competition cycle, so the durable skill is recognizing content by what it does rather than by its label.

The table below maps the numbered sections of the Notice of Funding Opportunity template to their legacy lettered equivalents and to the decision each one drives.

| Numbered section | Legacy letter | What the section decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Basic Information | Overview and B | Award size, number of awards, Assistance Listing Number, key dates |
| 2. Eligibility | C | Whether you may apply at all, and cost sharing |
| 3. Program Description | A | Agency priorities, performance expectations, statutory authority |
| 4. Application Contents and Format | D | Page limits, fonts, margins, required attachments, ordering |
| 5. Submission Requirements and Deadlines | D | Portal, due dates and times, intergovernmental review |
| 6. Application Review Information | E | Responsiveness rules, scoring criteria and weights, selection |
| 7. Award Notices | F | What a selection letter does and does not authorize |
| 8. Post-Award Requirements | G | Reporting cadence, terms and conditions, audit exposure |

Section 8 and the optional other-information section are where post-award obligations first appear, and they are load-bearing for a pursuit decision even though they contain nothing scorable.

## How do you read a NOFO step by step?

Read a Notice of Funding Opportunity in reverse dependency order: what wins, then what is required, then whether you qualify, then what the agency wants. Reading front to back produces enthusiasm before it produces facts, and enthusiasm is what makes an organization spend eighty hours on a competition it was never eligible for.

There are seven steps in the backward read:

- **Step 1 — Read the responsiveness rules.** The application review section must list "a list and enough detail to understand the criteria or disqualifying factors to be reviewed" ([Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/appendix-Appendix%20I%20to%20Part%20200)). These are binary. Page overruns, missing forms, and exceeding the cap on applications per entity end the process before review.
- **Step 2 — Extract the scoring criteria and weights.** Copy each criterion and sub-criterion verbatim into a working document, with its point value or percentage beside it.
- **Step 3 — Read eligibility and cost sharing.** Confirm entity type, any restriction on the number of applications, and whether a non-federal match is required. A match you cannot document is a disqualifier.
- **Step 4 — Extract every application content and format requirement.** Page limits, font, margins, paper size, color limits, file naming, attachment list, required ordering.
- **Step 5 — Extract every date.** Submission deadline, any letter of intent or pre-application date, the questions period, and the separate registration prerequisites.
- **Step 6 — Read the program description last.** Agency priorities, performance indicators, and the authorizing statute cited there tell you what the criteria mean in practice.
- **Step 7 — Build the requirements checklist.** One row per requirement, with the section it came from, the person responsible, and a compliance status. That checklist becomes the [compliance matrix](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/compliance-matrix/) that governs production.

The output of the backward read is not a decision to apply. It is the evidence a [go/no-go decision](/encyclopedia/finding-funding/go-no-go-decision/) needs, assembled before anyone has written a sentence of narrative.

## Where does the scoring live in a NOFO?

Scoring in a Notice of Funding Opportunity is distributed, not centralized. The review section carries the nominal rubric, and Appendix I requires that "if criteria vary in importance, the relative percentages, weights, or other means used to distinguish between them" be disclosed ([Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/appendix-Appendix%20I%20to%20Part%20200)). That disclosure is the single most useful sentence in the document.

Weights should drive page allocation directly. If project design carries forty percent of available points and organizational capacity carries ten, a narrative that gives each three pages is misallocated by design, and no amount of writing quality corrects it. Convert weights to page budgets before drafting, then hold the budget.

Three other locations carry scorable or decisive content. The **program description** states agency funding priorities and expected performance goals, indicators, targets, and baseline data; reviewers are frequently instructed to score against those even when the review section does not restate them. The **authorizing statute** cited in the program description sets purposes reviewers are obliged to weigh. And the **selection process** subsection discloses "any program policy, factors, or elements that the selecting official may use in selecting applications for the award. For example, geographical dispersion, program balance, or diversity."

Program policy factors deserve particular attention because they operate after scoring. Reviewers score; the selecting official selects. A top-scored application can be passed over for geographic balance, and the practice is legitimate precisely because the announcement disclosed it. Appendix I also requires disclosure of the agency's risk review, a separate pre-award evaluation of "financial stability," "quality of management systems," "history of performance," and "reports and findings from audits" ([2 CFR 200.206](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/subpart-C/section-200.206)). Merit and risk are scored separately, and an organization can pass one and fail the other.

## Where do page limits and formatting rules hide in a NOFO?

Page limits and formatting rules live in the application contents and format section, which must state "limitations on page numbers" and "formatting requirements, including font and font size, margins, paper size, and color limitations" ([Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/appendix-Appendix%20I%20to%20Part%20200)). Finding the rule is easy; finding all of it is not.

Formatting requirements in a Notice of Funding Opportunity fragment across at least four places. The application section states the general rule. Agency-wide application guides, incorporated by reference, often override it. Individual attachment instructions add their own limits. And the electronic forms themselves impose character counts that no prose section mentions. A compliant narrative can still fail because an abstract field truncated at a character limit nobody read.

Three questions resolve most ambiguity. First, does the page limit apply to the narrative only, or to the narrative plus appendices, résumés, and letters? Second, are charts, tables, and references inside or outside the count? Third, what happens on overrun — is the excess discarded, or is the application rejected? The answers are usually stated, and where they are not, the questions period exists to get them in writing.

Treat every formatting rule as a responsiveness rule until proven otherwise. Agencies vary in whether they enforce mechanically or forgivingly, and the applicant has no way to know which reviewer sees the file. Building the format constraints into the document template on day one costs nothing; retrofitting them the night before a deadline costs a rewrite.

## How do NOFO deadlines and registration deadlines work?

A Notice of Funding Opportunity carries several clocks, and only one of them is the submission deadline. The submission requirements section must state "due dates and times for all submissions" and distinguish electronic from paper submissions where they differ ([Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/appendix-Appendix%20I%20to%20Part%20200)). The clocks that end pursuits, however, are usually upstream of it.

Registration is the first clock. Federal applicants need an active entity registration before an application can be transmitted, and activation is measured in business days, not minutes; see the current figures above. Registration also expires annually, so an organization that applied in a prior cycle is not automatically able to apply in the next one. The mechanics are covered in [SAM.gov registration and the UEI](/encyclopedia/readiness/sam-gov-and-uei-registration/).

Transmission is the second clock, and it is not instantaneous. A federal application moves through a sequence of statuses after submission, and only a validated application is available for the agency to download; a package that fails validation is "Rejected with Errors," meaning the system "was unable to process your application because of an error(s) and cannot accept the application until you correct the error(s)" ([Grants.gov, Check Application Status](https://www.grants.gov/help/applicants/check-application-status)). Submitting at the deadline and discovering a validation error afterward produces a failed application, not a late one.

Agency-specific clocks are the third layer. NIH applications are "due by 5:00 PM local time of applicant organization on the specified due date," and standard due dates map each cycle to a scientific merit review month, an advisory council round, and an earliest possible project start date ([NIH, Standard Due Dates](https://grants.nih.gov/node/1091)). A deadline falling on a weekend, federal holiday, or Washington-area federal office closure extends automatically to the next business day ([NIH, Submission Policies](https://grants.nih.gov/grants-process/submit/submission-policies)).

NSF publishes the other end of the same problem. The agency states that "proposers have a minimum of 90 days from NSF's announcement of a funding opportunity to prepare and submit a proposal," and that it "strives to be able to tell applicants whether their proposals have been declined or recommended for funding within six months" ([NSF, Proposal and Award Process Timeline](https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/files/PAPPG-24-1-ex3_1_0.pdf)).

## What does a NOFO's program description reveal that the criteria do not?

The program description of a Notice of Funding Opportunity reveals the agency's unstated preferences — the shape of project it expects, the scale it can afford, and the statutory purpose it must serve. Review criteria describe how proposals are graded. The program description describes what the agency is trying to buy, and the two are not identical.

Award arithmetic is the fastest signal. Total anticipated funding divided by the anticipated number of awards, checked against the stated award range, tells you whether the agency is signaling many small awards or a few large ones ([2 CFR 200.204](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/subpart-C/section-200.204) requires all three figures in the posted summary). A budget at the top of the range in a competition making many small awards is a liability that appears in no criterion.

Cost constraints hide here too. The program description is where program-specific unallowable costs and statutory indirect cost limits appear. A statutory cap overrides an organization's negotiated indirect rate for that program, and budgeting at the negotiated rate against a capped program produces a budget-review kickback rather than a scoring penalty — a slower and more damaging failure.

The Assistance Listing is the description's companion document. Pulling the program's listing on SAM.gov surfaces the program description, eligibility, and audit applicability determination, plus historical obligation data the announcement usually omits ([SAM.gov, Assistance Listings](https://sam.gov/content/assistance-listings)). Reading the current announcement against the prior year's version is the other high-yield move: changed language in the priorities paragraph reflects new statutory direction or a new office director, and it is the most predictive text in the document. Locating the announcement in the first place is covered in [how to search for grants](/encyclopedia/finding-funding/how-to-search-for-grants/).

## What goes wrong when people read a NOFO?

Notice of Funding Opportunity reading fails in a small number of repeated ways, and each has a mechanical fix. Six are worth designing against:

- **Reading front to back.** The program description is persuasive and the eligibility rules are not, so front-to-back reading produces commitment before qualification.
- **Ignoring the weights.** Page allocation that does not track point allocation systematically underfunds the highest-scoring criterion.
- **Missing the responsiveness list.** Binary disqualifiers are stated plainly and skipped routinely because they read as boilerplate.
- **Treating the submission deadline as the only deadline.** Registration lapse and validation failure end more applications than weak narratives do.
- **Skipping the questions period.** Written agency answers are the only binding clarification available, and the window closes well before the deadline.
- **Failing to check the prior-year announcement.** Changed priority language is the clearest available statement of what the agency now wants.

The compounding failure is treating the announcement as a description rather than a contract. Every requirement in a Notice of Funding Opportunity is a term the applicant agrees to, and the ones in the post-award section govern years of obligation after the award is signed.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a NOFO and an FOA?

Notice of Funding Opportunity and Funding Opportunity Announcement name the same document. Agencies also use Request for Applications, Program Announcement, and Solicitation. The governing content requirements come from Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200 regardless of the label, and the announcement is indexed on Grants.gov by its funding opportunity number ([Grants.gov, Search Grants](https://www.grants.gov/search-grants)).

### Which section of a NOFO has the review criteria?

Review criteria appear in the application review information section — number 6 in the current template, Section E in the legacy lettered scheme. That section must also disclose relative weights where criteria differ in importance, the responsiveness or disqualification rules, the selection process, and the agency's risk review.

### What is a responsiveness review?

A responsiveness review is a pass or fail screen applied before merit review. Typical grounds are ineligible entity type, exceeding a page limit, missing required forms, or exceeding a cap on applications per applicant. Appendix I requires announcements to list these disqualifying factors with enough detail to understand them.

### How long is a federal grant application open?

Federal agencies should make opportunities available for at least 60 calendar days, and no opportunity should be open for fewer than 30 calendar days unless the agency determines exigent circumstances justify it. See the current figures above; forecast records are how applicants gain planning time beyond that window.

### What is the questions-and-answers period for?

The questions period converts private assumptions into written, published agency answers that bind the competition. Use it for ambiguities that change scope or cost — whether a partner counts as a subrecipient, whether a cost category is allowable, whether appendices count against the page limit — not for questions the announcement already answers.

### Can a high-scoring application still lose?

Yes, and legitimately. Announcements disclose program policy factors that the selecting official may apply after review, including geographic dispersion, program balance, and portfolio diversity. Reviewers produce scores; the selecting official makes awards, within the disclosed factors.
