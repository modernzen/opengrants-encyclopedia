# Goals, Objectives, and Activities

A goal is the broad direction a project moves in, stated as an aim rather than a measurement. An objective is a specific, measurable change the project commits to producing. Activities are the work performed, outputs are what the work produces, and outcomes are what changes for participants.

## Key takeaways

- A goal is direction; an objective is a destination.
- Funders buy objectives, not goals.
- Outputs are what you do; outcomes are what changes.
- SMART checks format, not whether the objective is right.
- Objectives written at proposal become reporting obligations after award.

## What is the difference between a goal and an objective?

A goal states the direction of a grant project in general terms; an objective states a specific, measurable change the project will produce. Goals are not observable. Objectives are, and they are what a funder is actually purchasing.

Lynn Miner's grantseeker's handbook draws the line sharply on both sides. Goals "represent the idealized dream of what you hope to accomplish… Goals are not observable or quantifiable." Objectives are the opposite: "Your objectives (sometimes called 'aims') specify the end products of your project. When sponsors fund your project, they are literally 'buying' your objectives… the objectives answer one fundamental question: 'What will this project change?'… If you can't see the change, it may not exist" ([University of Hawai'i Office of Research Services, Grantseeker's Handbook](https://research.hawaii.edu/files/ors/ORS_Grantseekers_Handbook.pdf)).

Swarthmore's sponsored programs office states the same distinction in one sentence each: "A goal is a general statement about the aim of a project," while objectives describe "the specific outcome of a project" and "can be indicated by a measurable change (increase or decrease) or a final product" ([Swarthmore College, Develop Your Proposal](https://www.swarthmore.edu/sponsored-programs/develop-your-proposal)). Within the [writing the proposal](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/) hub, that split is what keeps the evaluation section from having nothing to measure.

The table below contrasts the two on the four properties reviewers use to tell them apart.

| Property | Goal | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| Observable | No | Yes |
| Typical verbs | Improve, promote, advocate, extend | Increase, reduce, identify, certify |
| Carries a number and a date | No | Yes |
| What it commits you to | A direction | A reported result |

The University of Michigan's guide adds a phrasing rule that resolves most borderline cases: objectives "should focus on outcome as opposed to process," so "to identify the candidate allele" is an objective while "to run several trials on samples" is a process step. The same guide recommends keeping the objective set small; see the current figures above ([University of Michigan, Proposal Writer's Guide](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nextprof-science/wp-content/uploads/sites/130/2018/04/3-University-of-Michigan-Proposal-Writers-Guide.pdf)).

## How does the chain from goal to impact work?

The chain runs goal, objective, activity, output, outcome, impact, and each link is a causal claim a reviewer can test. A grant objective sits second in that sequence, translating a directional goal into a measurable commitment, and everything downstream either delivers it or measures it.

There are six links, and confusing any two of them costs points:

- **Goal** — the directional aim. Broad, unquantified, usually one per project.
- **Objective** — the measurable change the project commits to, with a population, a magnitude, a baseline, and a date.
- **Activity** — the work performed. St. Catherine University's definition is the compact one: "Activities are the means to achieving each objective; they are the work to be done" ([St. Catherine University, Proposal Development](https://www.stkate.edu/academics/research/spree/proposal-development)).
- **Output** — the countable product of activity. The W.K. Kellogg Foundation defines outputs as "the direct products of program activities," including "types, levels and targets of services to be delivered by the program" ([Logic Model Development Guide](https://www.naccho.org/uploads/downloadable-resources/Programs/Public-Health-Infrastructure/KelloggLogicModelGuide_161122_162808.pdf)).
- **Outcome** — the change in participants. Kellogg defines outcomes as "specific changes in program participants' behavior, knowledge, skills, status and level of functioning." The shortest available test comes from the evaluation literature: "outputs relate to what we do, while outcomes refer to what difference it makes" ([Pennsylvania Coalition Against Rape, Theory of Change and Logic Models](https://pcar.org/sites/default/files/resource-pdfs/tab_2018_logic_models_508.pdf)).
- **Impact** — the long-horizon condition change, which Kellogg describes as "fundamental intended or unintended change occurring in organizations, communities or systems."

Kellogg supplies the connective tissue that makes the sequence a logic rather than a list: "If you have access to [resources], then you can use them to accomplish your planned activities. If you accomplish your planned activities, then you will hopefully deliver the amount of product and/or service that you intended." Each tier of the chain carries its own expected time horizon; see the current figures above. The [outputs, outcomes, and impact](/encyclopedia/evidence/outputs-vs-outcomes/) reference treats the middle of that chain in more depth, including how to classify borderline measures.

## What do goals, objectives, and activities look like in practice?

Threading one project through all six levels is the fastest way to see where the distinctions bite. The illustration below uses a hypothetical third-grade literacy program; every figure in it is invented to show the shape of the chain, not drawn from any dataset.

| Level | Statement |
|---|---|
| Goal | Improve reading proficiency for elementary students in the target district |
| Objective | By the end of Year 2, 70 percent of the 120 enrolled third-graders will gain at least one grade level on the district reading assessment, up from a cohort baseline of 41 percent |
| Activities | Small-group tutoring three times weekly at 45 minutes per session; biweekly teacher coaching; monthly family literacy sessions |
| Outputs | 120 students enrolled; 4,320 tutoring sessions delivered; 36 coaching cycles completed |
| Outcomes | Reading-level gains at the stated rate; increased instructional minutes on targeted skills; sustained attendance in tutoring |
| Impact | District-wide third-grade proficiency converges toward the state rate |

Four things are visible in the illustration that are invisible in prose. The objective carries a baseline, so "ambitious" can be assessed — federal regulation defines a baseline as "the starting point from which performance is measured and targets are set," and judges whether a target is ambitious "upon the context of the relevant performance measure and the baseline for that measure" ([34 CFR 77.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/subtitle-A/part-77/section-77.1)). The activities carry dosage, so a reviewer can check whether the volume of service could plausibly produce the gain. The outputs are fully within staff control. And the impact is stated as convergence rather than attribution, because a single program cannot claim a district-wide effect it never measured against a counterfactual.

An objective written without a baseline forfeits all of that. The target floats, the evaluation has no comparison point, and the reviewer has no basis on which to judge ambition in either direction.

## Are SMART objectives a good standard for grant objectives?

SMART is a useful format check for a grant objective and a poor quality check. The acronym — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound — reliably catches objectives that lack a number or a date. It cannot tell you whether the objective is the right one, whether it follows from the documented need, or whether it is appropriately ambitious.

The critique is not folklore. A narrative review in *Health Psychology Review* found that the SMART acronym "is not based on scientific theory; is not consistent with empirical evidence; does not consider what type of goal is set; is not applied consistently; is lacking detailed guidance; has redundancy in its criteria; is not being used as originally intended; and has a risk of potentially harmful effects." The authors call on professional organizations "to cease the wholesale, uncritical dissemination of the SMART acronym" ([Swann et al., Health Psychology Review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2021.2023608); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35094640/)). A specific failure mode matters for grant writing: interpreting "A" as achievable and "R" as realistic pushes writers toward conservative targets, while the underlying goal-setting evidence favors challenging ones.

The practical reconciliation is to keep SMART and add what it omits. Many funder rubrics literally ask for specificity, measurability, and a timeframe, so an objective failing SMART will be marked down regardless of its merits. Miner's alternative mnemonic supplies the missing property explicitly — the "L" in SIMPLE stands for logical, defined as describing "how each objective systematically contributes to achieving your overall goal(s)." Logical connection is the property reviewers actually score, and it is the one SMART does not name.

The honest summary: SMART is a checklist, not a theory. Run it last, after the objective set has been derived from the need and the design, never first as a generator.

## How do reviewers test grant objectives for internal consistency?

Reviewers test grant objectives by reading them against the need, the activities, the budget, and the evaluation plan, and by noticing what appears in one place and not the others. The check is rarely formal; inconsistency registers as friction, and friction is scored as a weakness across several criteria at once.

Four traces are checked routinely. **Objective to need** — every objective should address a documented element of the [statement of need](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/writing-a-needs-statement/); an objective with no corresponding need is scope creep. **Activity to objective** — every activity in the [project design](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/project-design-and-methodology/) should serve a named objective; an activity with no objective is budget padding. **Objective to measure** — every objective should appear in the evaluation plan in the words the objective used, not as a convenient proxy. **Objective to budget** — every named role and activity should carry a line, and nothing else should.

Agencies say this directly. NSF's third merit review element asks: "Is the plan for carrying out the proposed activities well-reasoned, well-organized, and based on a sound rationale? Does the plan incorporate a mechanism to assess success?" ([NSF PAPPG, Chapter III](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-3-proposal-processing-review)). Education Department criteria ask reviewers to weigh "the quality of the logic model or other conceptual framework underlying the proposed project, including how inputs are related to outcomes" ([34 CFR 75.210](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/75.210)).

A synthesis of NSF decline reasons names the consistency failure as its own category, distinct from weak framing and weak methods: "Sound theoretical framework, solid methodology, but they don't align with each other" ([UC Irvine Research Development, How NSF Makes Awards](https://www.researchdevelopment.socsci.uci.edu/files/documents/2021/How%20NSF%20Makes%20Awards.pdf)). Two strong sections that do not connect score worse than two adequate sections that do.

## What do grant objectives commit you to after the award?

Grant objectives written at proposal stage become the reporting obligation after award. The numbers a writer chose to sound competitive are the numbers a program director will be asked to produce, on a fixed schedule, for the life of the award.

Federal performance reporting is regulated rather than negotiated. Agencies must collect performance information on a set cadence with fixed submission deadlines, including a final report after the period of performance ends; see the current figures above ([2 CFR 200.329](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.329)). Missed targets do not automatically end an award, but they generate explanations, corrective action plans, and — for recipients with a pattern — heightened scrutiny in future risk reviews.

Two habits protect against over-promising without making a proposal look timid. First, anchor each target to three things: the effect size in the studies cited for the approach, the organization's own historical trend, and the distance to the benchmark. A target justified by all three is defensible in review and survivable in year two. Second, pair any target that can only be observed after the grant ends with a leading indicator that will move inside the period of performance, and say plainly which is which.

The asymmetry is worth stating: an objective set slightly too conservative costs points in one review. An objective set substantially too aggressive costs credibility with the same program office across every subsequent application. The [evaluation plan](/encyclopedia/evidence/writing-an-evaluation-plan/) is where that promise gets operationalized, which is why the two sections should be drafted together.

## What goes wrong with grant goals and objectives?

Grant goals and objectives fail in a predictable set of ways, and each is visible in a draft. Six recur:

- **Objectives that restate the goal.** "To improve literacy outcomes for participating students" is a direction with no number, no population, and no date. Repair: add magnitude, baseline, population, and deadline.
- **Process statements written as objectives.** "To study," "to explore," and "to provide services" describe work, not change. Repair: use outcome verbs — identify, increase, reduce, certify.
- **Outputs presented as outcomes.** "500 people served" counts activity, not benefit. Repair: apply the controllability test — a result fully within staff control is an output.
- **Too many objectives.** A long list dilutes the evaluation plan and multiplies reporting obligations. Repair: keep the set small enough that each one earns its measurement cost.
- **Orphan activities.** Work described in the design that serves no stated objective. Repair: cut it, or promote the objective it implicitly serves.
- **Targets without baselines.** A promised gain with no starting value cannot be judged ambitious or unambitious. Repair: state the baseline, its source, and its measurement date.

A seventh failure is structural rather than editorial: writing objectives before the need statement is finished. Objectives derived from what an organization wants to do, then retrofitted to a need, produce exactly the misalignment reviewers are trained to look for.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many objectives should a grant proposal have?

Few enough that each one can be measured and reported. The University of Michigan's proposal guide recommends a small set of outcome objectives per proposal; see the current figures above. Funder instructions override any general rule, and some competitions specify a required count or a required format.

### What is the difference between an output and an outcome?

An output is the countable product of an activity and is fully within staff control; an outcome is the change participants experience. Number of workshops delivered is an output. Whether participants gained knowledge is an outcome. The distinction is covered in depth in outputs, outcomes, and impact.

### Can a goal be measurable?

It can, but it usually should not be. Making the goal measurable typically means it has become an objective, which leaves the proposal with no statement of direction and a set of measures with no organizing aim. Keep the goal directional and put all measurement in the objectives.

### Do foundations use the same goal and objective vocabulary?

Mostly, with looser enforcement. Foundation applications often ask for goals and expected results rather than formal objectives, and rarely require baselines. The underlying test is unchanged: a reviewer must be able to tell what will be different, for whom, and by when.

### What is a logic model's relationship to objectives?

A logic model lays out inputs, activities, outputs, and outcomes in sequence, and the objectives are the outcome commitments drawn from it. Building the [logic model](/encyclopedia/evidence/logic-models/) first usually produces cleaner objectives, because the chain forces every promised change to have activities behind it.

### Are SMART objectives required by federal funders?

Not by regulation. Federal criteria ask for specific, measurable targets connected to strategies, activities, resources, outputs, and outcomes, which SMART approximates but does not define. Where a notice of funding opportunity names SMART explicitly, follow the notice; where it does not, meet the underlying criterion.
