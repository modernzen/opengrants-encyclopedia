# Writing a Statement of Need

A statement of need documents a gap between conditions as they are and conditions as they should be, for a named population in a named geography, using data at that geography's scale. The need belongs to the community or the problem, never to the applicant organization.

## Key takeaways

- A need is two numbers and the distance between them.
- The need must exist whether or not your organization does.
- Local data beats national data for locally delivered programs.
- Unserved capacity, not just prevalence, is what makes a gap.
- Reviewers check whether the need and the design match.

## What is a statement of need in a grant proposal?

A statement of need is the section of a grant proposal that establishes a documented discrepancy between current conditions and a defensible standard, for a defined population in a defined place. Funders also call it the problem statement, the need to be addressed, the situation description, or — in research proposals — significance.

The controlling technical definition comes from Lynn Miner's grantseeker's handbook: "By definition, a problem or need is a gap--a discrepancy between the way things are and the way things ought to be. To persuade proposal reviewers that you have identified a significant problem, you must present two data points or lines that are separated by a gap" ([University of Hawai'i Office of Research Services, Grantseeker's Handbook](https://research.hawaii.edu/files/ors/ORS_Grantseekers_Handbook.pdf)). A single statistic is not a need. Two statistics and the space between them is a need.

Placement matters as much as content. Swarthmore's sponsored programs office advises applicants to "place your needs statement near the beginning of the proposal so that reviewers immediately understand why the project is important" ([Swarthmore College, Develop Your Proposal](https://www.swarthmore.edu/sponsored-programs/develop-your-proposal)). In the standard [anatomy of a grant proposal](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/anatomy-of-a-grant-proposal/), the need section is the first burden of proof and the foundation for every section after it, which is why the rest of the [writing the proposal](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/) hub treats it as load-bearing.

Federal reviewers are given an explicit instruction about what a need statement must contain. The Department of Education's selection criteria ask for "the data presented (including a comparison to local, State, regional, national, or international data) that demonstrates the issue, challenge, or opportunity to be addressed by the proposed project" ([34 CFR 75.210](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/75.210)). Comparison is not decoration; it is the criterion.

## Whose need does a statement of need describe?

A statement of need describes the community's condition, not the applicant organization's resource shortfall. The Grantsmanship Center identifies this as the single persistent error: organizations "write about what their organization needs instead of what the community needs," and states the rule in one line — "Remember: the absence of your program is not the need" ([The Grantsmanship Center, What Is a Needs Statement?](https://www.tgci.com/blog/2026/06/what-needs-statement)).

The City of Albuquerque's grant toolkit gives the clearest worked contrast. An organization "may need a van to address the need, but you should not detail why your organization needs a van. Instead, talk about the fact that there is no after-school program at the partner school and the nearest one is at a community center that is several miles away" ([City of Albuquerque, Application Components: Need Statement](https://www.cabq.gov/office-of-neighborhood-coordination/neighborhood-toolbox/application-components-need-statement)). The van is a budget line. The distance to the nearest program is a need.

One test resolves almost every case: would the problem still be describable if your organization vanished tomorrow? If the answer is no, the draft describes a want. Swarthmore names the same failure as a logical error rather than a framing error — "avoid circular logic, in which the lack of a solution is the problem."

Describing community need does not require describing organizational weakness. The University of Michigan's proposal guide recommends the opposite posture: "Positively represent your capabilities, e.g., 'We have a strong academic program, but we want to reach more students' vs 'We do not have any resources'" ([University of Michigan, Proposal Writer's Guide](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nextprof-science/wp-content/uploads/sites/130/2018/04/3-University-of-Michigan-Proposal-Writers-Guide.pdf)). Capacity belongs in the capacity section; scarcity language in the need section reads as a request for rescue.

## How do you write a statement of need step by step?

Writing a statement of need follows six moves, in order, each answering a question a skeptical reviewer will ask next. The Grantsmanship Center sets out the same sequence: establish the problem, show it locally with data, describe who is affected and at what scale, explain the consequences of inaction, document why existing resources fall short, and connect the need to the proposed program.

There are six steps:

- **Step 1 — Define the population and the geography precisely.** Name the age band, the eligibility condition, the schools, the tracts, the county, or the service area. A vague population makes every subsequent number unverifiable.
- **Step 2 — Establish magnitude with data at that geography's scale.** Report how many people are affected, not merely that many are. Cite the dataset, the table, and the vintage rather than "census data."
- **Step 3 — Establish the gap.** Pair the current condition with the standard, benchmark, or comparison geography, so the reader can see the distance. The Department of Education's need criterion asks for exactly this comparison.
- **Step 4 — Establish the consequences of inaction.** State what worsens, for whom, and over what horizon. Albuquerque's toolkit frames the three qualities to demonstrate as important, significant, and urgent — real implications, large scope, and a reason to act before the condition deteriorates.
- **Step 5 — Establish why existing services do not close the gap.** Count the providers, count the slots, count the people served, and subtract. Unserved demand is the part applicants under-write most often, and it is the part that makes the request non-duplicative.
- **Step 6 — Connect the gap to what you propose, without describing the project.** One or two sentences naming which part of the gap the project addresses. The mechanics belong in the [project design](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/project-design-and-methodology/).

The Grantsmanship Center offers a discipline for each step: the statement "should be able to hold up to the question 'So what?' at every step." A sentence that survives that question stays; a sentence that does not is consuming a page limit.

## What data belongs in a statement of need?

Statement of need data should match the geography being served, carry a named source and vintage, and include at least one comparison value. Reviewers read a specific table citation as a competence signal and a bare "according to census data" as its opposite.

Three federal sources cover most needs statements. The **American Community Survey** supplies income, poverty, educational attainment, employment, disability, housing cost burden, and internet access; the estimate type you may use depends on the population size of your area, and small areas require the multi-year file — see the current figures above ([Census Bureau, When to Use 1-year or 5-year Estimates](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/guidance/estimates.html)). **Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates** supply annual poverty and median income figures down to the school district, which is the standard citation for education need ([Census Bureau, About SAIPE](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/saipe/about.html)). **CDC PLACES** supplies small-area estimates for chronic disease, prevention practices, disability, and health-related social needs at the census tract level ([CDC, About PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/about/index.html)).

Two properties of these datasets must be disclosed rather than hidden. SAIPE and PLACES are model-based: the Census Bureau states that SAIPE estimates "are not direct counts from enumerations or administrative records, nor direct estimates from sample surveys." And small-area survey estimates carry margins of error large enough to matter. Naming both is a rigor signal; presenting a modeled tract estimate as a precise count is the kind of error a reviewer with statistical training will notice and mark.

National statistics have exactly one legitimate role in a locally delivered program: as the comparison line. A national prevalence rate establishes what normal looks like; the local rate establishes the gap. A needs statement built only from national figures has described a problem that exists somewhere, which is not the same as a problem that exists here. The [public data sources for needs statements](/encyclopedia/evidence/data-sources-for-needs-statements/) reference covers the wider directory, including labor market, housing, education, food access, and broadband sources.

## How do you use stories in a statement of need without distorting?

Stories in a statement of need illustrate a documented condition; they do not establish it. The safe construction is quantitative first, qualitative second: the data establishes scale and the gap, then a short, specific, consented account shows what the gap looks like at human resolution.

Three rules keep the combination honest. **Do not generalize from the case.** One participant's experience is evidence that the experience occurs, not evidence of its frequency; the frequency claim needs the data. **Do not select for extremity.** A story chosen because it is the worst case misrepresents the population the program will actually serve, and misrepresentation surfaces later in performance reporting. **Do not use a story to fill a hole in the evidence.** Where data does not exist, say so plainly and describe the collection you will do, rather than substituting narrative for measurement.

Consent and dignity are proposal ethics, not decoration. Use pseudonyms unless permission is explicit, remove identifying detail that serves no evidentiary purpose, and describe people as they would describe themselves. A reviewer who reads a participant as a prop discounts the entire section.

Qualitative evidence has a legitimate independent function that statistics cannot serve: it documents barriers that administrative data never records. Waiting-list interviews, provider surveys, focus groups, and 211 call volumes can establish that demand exists in a form no dataset captures. Presented that way — as evidence of unmet demand rather than as emotional support for a claim already made — testimony strengthens the gap argument rather than diluting it.

## What does weak versus strong need statement phrasing look like?

Weak need statement phrasing asserts; strong phrasing documents a gap for a specific population in a specific place. The table below contrasts four common weak constructions with the repaired version, using bracketed placeholders in place of the figures a real draft would carry.

| Weak construction | Repaired construction | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| "Mental health challenges affect millions of Americans." | "In [County], [X] percent of adults reported symptoms of depression or anxiety in the past year, yet only [Y] percent received treatment." | National becomes local; one number becomes a gap |
| "Our organization is committed to expanding after-school programming." | "In [City], [X] percent of school-age children are unsupervised after school, and the nearest provider is [N] miles from the target school." | Organizational aspiration becomes community condition |
| "There is a need for a mentoring program like ours." | "[X] youth aged 12–17 in [tract] have no adult mentor; the county's [N] providers together serve [Y], leaving [X minus Y] unserved." | Circular logic becomes counted unserved demand |
| "Food insecurity is a serious problem in our community." | "In [County], the child food insecurity rate is [X] percent against a state rate of [Y] percent, and fewer than [Z] percent of eligible households are enrolled in [program]." | Adjective becomes benchmark plus service gap |

Two of these repairs follow the pattern The Grantsmanship Center recommends: replace organizational aspiration with community condition, and add the second data point that turns a prevalence figure into a gap. The third removes the circularity Swarthmore warns against. The fourth adds the benchmark that federal need criteria ask for by name.

More evidence is not automatically better. The Grantsmanship Center observes that a frequent problem in needs statements "is not lack of evidence but too much of it." Four well-chosen, well-sourced numbers that build one argument outperform fourteen that build none. Sentence construction compounds the effect: NIH advises applicants that "sentences of 20 words or less are easier to read," and a need statement is the section most prone to clause-stacking ([NIH, General Grant Writing Tips](https://grants.nih.gov/grants-process/write-application/general-grant-writing-tips)).

## How do reviewers test a statement of need against the project design?

Reviewers test a statement of need by reading it against the rest of the proposal and looking for elements that appear in one and not the other. The test is not formal; it registers as friction, and friction costs points across several criteria at once.

Three mismatches are checked routinely. **A need with no corresponding activity** signals that the section was written to be impressive rather than operative — a needs statement that documents housing instability, transportation barriers, and food insecurity, followed by a design that addresses only tutoring, invites the question of why the other two were raised. **An objective with no corresponding need** is scope creep, and it usually means the design came first and the need was retrofitted. **A population defined one way in the need and another way in the design** — county-wide in the need, three schools in the design — makes every magnitude figure in the need section inapplicable to the project being funded.

The Grantsmanship Center proposes a readback test that catches most of these before submission. After reading the needs statement alone, could a reviewer accurately describe the problem, who it affects, how many people are involved, why existing resources are insufficient, and why this community specifically needs this intervention? Any "no" is a rewrite, not an edit.

Baselines are where the need section reaches forward into evaluation. Federal regulation defines a baseline as "the starting point from which performance is measured and targets are set," and defines an ambitious target relative to that baseline ([34 CFR 77.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/subtitle-A/part-77/section-77.1)). A needs statement that establishes the current condition precisely is also supplying the baseline that makes every later target assessable. One written well does double duty; one written vaguely damages two sections.

## What goes wrong in a statement of need?

Statement of need drafting fails in a small set of ways, and each has a mechanical repair. Six recur across public and private funders:

- **Circular need.** The absence of the proposed program is presented as the problem. Repair: describe the condition the program would change, measured independently of the program.
- **The need for money.** Budget shortfall substituted for community condition. Repair: move the resource discussion to capacity and budget, where it belongs.
- **Unsupported assertion.** Adjectives — serious, alarming, growing — with no cited figure behind them. Repair: one number and one source per claim, or delete the claim.
- **Data at the wrong unit of analysis.** State or national figures used for a program delivered in three neighborhoods. Repair: pull the smallest reliable geography available, and use the larger geography as the comparison line.
- **Stale or undated data.** A figure with no vintage cannot be evaluated and cannot be updated. Repair: name the dataset, the table, and the reference period every time.
- **Solution creep.** The project described inside the need section, which then leaves the design section repeating itself. Repair: the need section ends at the gap; the design section starts there.

A seventh failure is quieter and more expensive: spending four pages on the problem and half a page on why existing services do not solve it. The unserved-demand argument is what distinguishes a fundable gap from a well-documented condition that other providers are already addressing, and it is the part reviewers use to judge whether the request is duplicative. When space is tight, that ratio should move the other way.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a statement of need be?

As long as the scoring weight justifies. In a point-weighted federal competition, allocate pages in proportion to the points assigned to the need criterion. In a foundation application with no published weights, one to two pages is typical, with the unserved-demand argument given roughly equal space to the prevalence argument.

### Can you use national data in a needs statement?

Yes, as the comparison line. National figures establish what typical looks like so the local figure can be read as a gap. A locally delivered program supported only by national statistics has not demonstrated that the problem exists in the service area, which is the claim the section is required to prove.

### What is the difference between a need and a want?

A need is a documented condition affecting a defined population that would persist if the applicant organization did not exist. A want is a resource the organization lacks. The Grantsmanship Center's formulation is the shortest available test: the absence of your program is not the need.

### Should the needs statement describe the proposed project?

Only in the final sentence or two, and only to connect the documented gap to what will be addressed. Describing activities, staffing, or dosage inside the need section duplicates the project design and consumes pages the design section needs for its own burden of proof.

### How recent does needs statement data have to be?

Recent enough that a reviewer would not object, and always dated. Federal small-area datasets release on fixed schedules, and multi-year survey estimates are pooled by design, so the reference period matters more than the release date. State the reference period explicitly rather than implying currency.

### Where does the needs statement connect to the logic model?

At the far left. The gap documented in the need section is the condition the [logic model](/encyclopedia/evidence/logic-models/) proposes to change through outcomes, which the W.K. Kellogg Foundation defines as "specific changes in program participants' behavior, knowledge, skills, status and level of functioning" ([Logic Model Development Guide](https://www.naccho.org/uploads/downloadable-resources/Programs/Public-Health-Infrastructure/KelloggLogicModelGuide_161122_162808.pdf)). A need that never reappears in the model is a need the project does not address.
