# Building a Compliance Matrix

A compliance matrix is a numbered register of every requirement stated in a funding announcement, each row carrying its source location, an assigned owner, and the exact place in the draft that satisfies it. The matrix converts scattered instructions into a list that someone can check line by line before submission.

## Key takeaways

- Compliance is binary and is judged before any reviewer scores the proposal.
- Extract every must, shall, will, and required into numbered rows.
- Formatting, file naming, and page limits are requirements, not preferences.
- Track submission mechanics on a separate register from narrative content.
- The final check belongs to someone who did not write the draft.

## What is a compliance matrix in a grant proposal?

A compliance matrix is a working document, not a deliverable. Within [writing the proposal](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/), the compliance matrix is the artifact that sits between reading the announcement and drafting anything: one row per stated requirement, extracted verbatim, numbered, and traceable back to the page it came from.

The discipline transfers from federal contracting, where solicitations separate instructions from evaluation. Under the Federal Acquisition Regulation, Section L carries "instructions, conditions, and notices to offerors," and Section M identifies "all significant factors and any significant subfactors that will be considered in awarding the contract and their relative importance" ([FAR 15.204-5](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/15.204-5)). Grant announcements carry the same two things under different names — application contents and review criteria — and the same crosswalk applies.

Federal announcements are required to be structured, which makes extraction tractable. Agencies must include the information specified in Appendix I of the Uniform Guidance in every funding opportunity, must display key dates and eligibility summary information ahead of the full text, and should keep opportunities open for at least 60 calendar days ([2 CFR 200.204](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.204)). Requirements are therefore predictable in location even when they are unpredictable in wording — the practical basis for [reading a NOFO](/encyclopedia/finding-funding/how-to-read-a-nofo/) systematically rather than sequentially.

Grant professionals treat requirement interpretation as a scored competency in its own right. The Grant Professional Certification Institute lists "interpret funding opportunity guidelines and requirements to ensure high-quality responses" as a discrete skill under crafting and submitting an application ([GPCI, *Competencies and Skills*](https://www.grantcredential.org/wp-content/uploads/GPC-Competencies-and-Skills.pdf)).

## Where does a compliance matrix fit in the three-layer test?

A compliance matrix operates on the first two of three layers that every proposal passes through in order: compliance, responsiveness, and competitiveness. Each layer is judged by different people, and failure at an earlier layer prevents the later ones from ever being reached.

The table below distinguishes the three layers by the question each asks and the consequence of failing it.

| Layer | Question it asks | Who judges it | Consequence of failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compliance | Was it submitted as required, on time, by an eligible applicant? | Administrative screeners and automated checks | Elimination before review, with no score |
| Responsiveness | Was the funder's actual question answered, in the funder's frame? | Program staff and reviewers | Scored, but low; unaddressed criteria earn nothing |
| Competitiveness | Was the answer better than the other answers? | Reviewers and portfolio decision-makers | Scored well and still declined |

Most applicant effort goes into the third layer. The first two are where preventable losses happen. NSF enumerates the grounds on which a proposal will not be accepted or will be returned without review, including that it "does not meet NSF proposal preparation requirements, such as page limitations, formatting instructions, and electronic submission," that it "is not responsive to the NSF funding opportunity," and that it "does not contain each of the required sections of the proposal" ([NSF PAPPG, Chapter IV.B](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-4-non-award-decisions-transactions)). Adherence to preparation requirements, NSF states, "is strictly enforced" ([NSF, *Overview of the Proposal and Award Process*](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/overview)).

Purdue University's writing lab states the rule in the terms an applicant should internalize: "Keep in mind that many grantmakers will not read past the point of your departure from the application rules, no matter how worthy the project is or how neat and well designed the application package looks… Ultimately, not following directions indicates carelessness—which is not a characteristic of a promising proposal" ([Purdue OWL, *Introduction to Grant Writing*](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/grant_writing/index.html)).

State the honest version rather than the folklore version. Compliance failures do not cause most declines — competition does. Compliance and responsiveness failures cause most *avoidable* declines, and they are the only category in which an applicant loses without ever being read.

## How do you build a compliance matrix from an announcement?

Build a compliance matrix in seven passes through the announcement, each pass looking for one class of requirement so that nothing is caught by accident. Work in a spreadsheet, not a document, because the register has to be sortable by owner and by status.

1. **Shred the mandatory language.** Extract every sentence containing must, shall, will, required, or may not. Each extracted sentence becomes one numbered row, quoted verbatim rather than paraphrased.
2. **Record the source for every row.** Capture the section name and page number of the announcement, plus any attachment or appendix the requirement lives in. A row without a source cannot be re-checked when the announcement is amended.
3. **Split compound requirements.** A single instruction such as "describe your approach to evaluation, including data sources, analysis methods, and reporting schedule" is four rows, not one. Compound rows are where partial answers hide.
4. **Overlay the review criteria on the application contents.** One content instruction frequently maps to several separately scored elements. The crosswalk between what to submit and how it will be scored is the highest-value pass in the process.
5. **Capture formatting constraints as first-class rows.** Page limits, margins, typeface and point size, line spacing, page numbering, file naming, and permitted file types each get their own row with their own owner.
6. **Capture submission mechanics on a separate register.** Portal, registration status, authorized submitter, and the deadline expressed in the announcement's stated time zone are not narrative requirements and should not compete with narrative rows for attention.
7. **Assign an owner and a location to every row.** Owner is a named person, not a department. Location is a section heading and eventually a page number in the draft.

Formatting rules are not soft. NSF's Chapter II specifies proposal font, spacing and margin requirements alongside pagination and page formatting rules ([NSF PAPPG, Chapter II](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-2-proposal-preparation)), and NSF has returned proposals for omitting literally labeled sections — a Project Summary must include separate sections labeled "Overview," "Intellectual Merit," and "Broader Impacts," and the Project Description must include a section "with a heading" for Broader Impacts ([NSF Dear Colleague Letter 15-095](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/information/dcl-communication-about-automated-compliance-checking-proposal/nsf15-095)).

Registration status belongs on the mechanics register from day one, because it is the requirement least amenable to a last-minute fix. Federal submission requires an active entity registration, and a lapse is not waivable at the deadline ([SAM.gov, *Entity Registration*](https://sam.gov/content/entity-registration)); the sequence is covered in [SAM.gov registration and the UEI](/encyclopedia/readiness/sam-gov-and-uei-registration/).

## What belongs in each compliance matrix row?

Each compliance matrix row carries six fields: requirement identifier, verbatim requirement text, source section and page, assigned owner, location in the draft, and status. Six fields is the working minimum — fewer, and the row cannot be verified by anyone except its author.

The worked matrix below shows five representative rows from a federal discretionary application, abbreviated for width.

| ID | Requirement (verbatim excerpt) | Source | Owner | Draft location | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-014 | "Applicants must describe the data sources used to establish need" | §IV.B, p. 18 | Program lead | Need, p. 4 | Drafted |
| R-015 | "The project narrative must not exceed 20 pages" | §IV.B, p. 17 | Proposal manager | Whole narrative | Verified |
| R-016 | "Each attachment must be submitted as a separate PDF" | §IV.C, p. 21 | Proposal manager | Upload package | Not started |
| R-017 | "Applicants shall provide a letter of commitment from each partner" | §IV.B, p. 19 | Executive director | Attachment 3 | In hand |
| R-018 | "Budget narrative must justify every personnel line" | §IV.D, p. 23 | Finance lead | Budget narrative | Drafted |

Status values should be few and unambiguous: not started, drafted, verified. "In progress" is not a status, because it tells the person doing the final check nothing they can act on.

One field is worth adding when the announcement publishes point weights: the points attached to the criterion a row serves. Weight is the instruction for page allocation, and a matrix that records it makes an over-invested section visible while there is still time to move pages.

## How does a responsiveness matrix differ from a compliance matrix?

A responsiveness matrix maps each published review criterion to the narrative section and page that addresses it, where a compliance matrix maps each stated requirement to the place it is satisfied. The compliance matrix answers "did we do what was asked"; the responsiveness matrix answers "can the reviewer find it."

Responsiveness has a formal existence, not just a rhetorical one. NSF treats "not responsive to the NSF funding opportunity" as a separate ground for return without review from failure to meet preparation requirements ([NSF PAPPG, Chapter IV.B](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-4-non-award-decisions-transactions)). A proposal can be perfectly formatted and still be returned for answering a question the funder did not ask.

Including a version of the responsiveness matrix in the proposal itself helps the reader, where the announcement permits it and it does not consume limited pages. The National Institutes of Health explains the underlying reason: "Reviewers are accustomed to finding information in specific sections… This creates an efficient evaluation process and saves reviewers from hunting" ([NIH, *General Grant Writing Tips*](https://grants.nih.gov/grants-process/write-application/general-grant-writing-tips)). A short crosswalk table near the front of a narrative does the same job as the headings, for the reviewer who arrives at the section out of order — a behavior described in [writing for the reviewer](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/writing-for-grant-reviewers/).

Responsiveness also has a vocabulary dimension. The University of Michigan's proposal guide instructs applicants to "respond directly to the priorities of the funder and make the connection clear," and warns against assuming "the sponsor will change the guidelines just because you have a good idea that falls outside of them" ([University of Michigan ORSP, *The Proposal Writer's Guide*](https://orsp.umich.edu/sites/default/files/resource-download/proposal-writers-guide-final.pdf)). Restating the funder's priorities back at them is a different error and equally costly, because it spends the reviewer's attention on words they wrote.

## Who runs the final compliance check before submission?

The final compliance check belongs to someone who did not write the draft, working from the matrix rather than from the document. Authors verify what they meant to write; a fresh reader verifies what is on the page. Separating those two roles is the entire mechanism.

Run the check as a distinct, calendared step with its own deadline, positioned far enough before submission that a failed row can still be fixed. Three days is a workable minimum for a federal application; the check itself takes hours, but remediation does not.

The final check covers five things in this order:

- **Every row resolved.** No row sits at "not started" or "drafted." Each is verified against the assembled package, not against the working draft.
- **Formatting measured, not eyeballed.** Page counts, margins, typeface, point size, and page numbering are checked against the announcement's stated values in the final compiled file.
- **File package assembled.** Attachment count, file types, file names, and any naming convention the announcement specifies.
- **Mechanics confirmed.** Registration active, authorized submitter available, portal credentials working, and the deadline read in the time zone the announcement names.
- **Responsiveness sampled.** For each scored criterion, the checker locates the page that answers it without using search.

Submitting early is part of the discipline rather than a personality trait. Portal validation errors surface only on submission, and a package rejected by a system on the closing day is indistinguishable in outcome from a package never sent. The supporting documents that most often hold up a submission are inventoried in [the documents every grant application needs](/encyclopedia/readiness/grant-documentation-file/).

## What goes wrong with compliance matrices?

Six failure modes account for most of the value lost from a compliance matrix, and every one of them is procedural rather than intellectual. Each converts an instrument built to prevent unforced errors into paperwork that documents them after the fact.

- **Built after drafting starts.** The matrix becomes a retrospective checklist and stops shaping what gets written or how much space it gets.
- **Paraphrased requirements.** Rows written in the author's words rather than the funder's lose the exact obligation and the exact vocabulary the reviewer expects.
- **Compound rows left whole.** One row covering four sub-requirements is marked complete when three are answered.
- **Formatting and mechanics buried among narrative rows.** File naming and registration status compete for attention with substantive content and lose.
- **Amendments not re-shredded.** The announcement is modified after posting, and the matrix continues to reflect the original text.
- **Checked by the author.** The person who wrote the section verifies the section, which tests memory rather than the document.

A seventh pattern appears on the funder side and is worth naming because a matrix can catch it. Foundations decline applications where "the resources required exceed" published funding guidelines or "appear to be mismatched with the project activities described" ([Tinker Foundation, *Why Was My Letter of Inquiry Declined?*](https://tinker.org/why-was-my-letter-of-inquiry-declined/)). Award ceilings, budget-period limits, and cost-share expectations are stated requirements and belong on the register alongside page limits — and the distinction between assistance instruments that carry these rules is set out in [grants vs contracts vs cooperative agreements](/encyclopedia/funding-tracks/grants-vs-contracts/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What words should you extract into a compliance matrix?

Extract every sentence containing must, shall, will, required, may not, or is prohibited. Add sentences that specify a number — page counts, word limits, attachment counts, budget ceilings, periods of performance. Directive verbs and quantities together capture nearly all binding requirements in a funding announcement.

### Should the compliance matrix go into the proposal?

The compliance matrix itself stays internal. A condensed responsiveness crosswalk — review criterion, narrative section, page number — can go into the proposal where the announcement permits it and pages allow. Never include a matrix that reveals unfinished rows or internal owners.

### How long does building a compliance matrix take?

Building a compliance matrix for a first-time federal announcement typically takes several hours of careful reading, and less on a repeat competition where prior rows can be reused. The time is recovered during drafting, because writers work from resolved requirements rather than re-reading the announcement.

### What if the announcement is ambiguous?

Ambiguous requirements become rows with an open question and a named owner for resolving it. Agencies must make clarifying information available to all potential applicants, typically by posting answers publicly, so questions asked early benefit the whole pool and produce a citable answer for the matrix.

### Does a compliance matrix help with private foundation applications?

A compliance matrix helps with foundation applications, though the register is shorter. Foundation guidelines carry page limits, attachment lists, budget ceilings, geographic and program restrictions, and portal mechanics. Foundations decline applications for exceeding published funding guidelines, which a register catches before the request is finalized.

### How much does formatting really matter?

Formatting carries the smallest weight in the profession's own scoring of grant writing — 4 percent of the Grant Professional Certified writing rubric, against 34 percent for persuasive argument ([GPCI, *Competencies Tested*](https://www.grantcredential.org/the-exam/competencies-tested/)). It matters disproportionately anyway, because formatting failures are screened before scoring and cost the entire application rather than a few points.
