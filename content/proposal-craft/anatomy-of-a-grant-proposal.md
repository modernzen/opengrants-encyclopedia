# The Anatomy of a Grant Proposal

A grant proposal is a set of sections that together answer six questions: is the problem real, does the proposed solution follow from it, can the applicant execute, will success be measured, is the budget justified, and does the work survive the grant?

## Key takeaways

- Section names change by funder; the burden of proof does not.
- Each section proves one thing a reviewer would otherwise doubt.
- Compliance gates end applications before any score is assigned.
- Page allocation should track published scoring weights.
- The abstract is drafted last and read first.

## What sections does a grant proposal contain?

Nearly every grant proposal contains the same eight components under different labels: a summary, a statement of need, goals and objectives, a project design, an organizational capacity section, an evaluation plan, a sustainability plan, and a budget with a narrative. Funders reorder and rename them, but the set is stable across public and private funding.

The federal version of that set is codified. Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200 requires every notice of funding opportunity to state its application contents, its review criteria, and its submission rules, which is why federal announcements converge on similar section lists ([Appendix I to 2 CFR Part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/appendix-Appendix%20I%20to%20Part%20200)). The U.S. Department of Education goes further and publishes a menu of general selection criteria — need for the project, quality of project design, quality of project personnel, adequacy of resources, quality of the management plan, and quality of the project evaluation — that program offices draw from when writing a competition ([34 CFR 75.210](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/75.210)).

Private funders compress the same material. A foundation application that asks for a two-page narrative is still asking for need, plan, capacity, measurement, and money; the sections have merged rather than disappeared. The rest of the [writing the proposal](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/) hub treats each section on its own terms, because each one is scored on its own terms.

Two components behave differently from the others. The summary is a compression of everything else, and the budget is the only section that must reconcile numerically with the narrative rather than merely agree with it in spirit.

## What is each grant proposal section asked to prove?

Each grant proposal section carries a distinct burden of proof, and reviewers evaluate whether that burden was met rather than whether the prose was pleasant. The need section proves a problem exists independent of the applicant. The design section proves the activities can produce the promised outcomes. Capacity proves prior execution. Evaluation proves failure would be visible.

There are six burdens, and they run in dependency order:

- **Is the problem real?** Carried by the [statement of need](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/writing-a-needs-statement/), which must document a gap using data at the scale of the population served, and benchmark it against comparison geographies ([34 CFR 75.210](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/75.210) asks for "a comparison to local, State, regional, national, or international data").
- **Does the solution follow from the problem?** Carried jointly by [goals and objectives](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/goals-and-objectives/) and the [project design](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/project-design-and-methodology/). An objective that does not trace to a documented element of the need is scope creep.
- **Can the applicant do it?** Carried by [organizational capacity](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/demonstrating-organizational-capacity/), which is an argument from track record, not from intent.
- **Will anyone know if it worked?** Carried by the [evaluation plan](/encyclopedia/evidence/writing-an-evaluation-plan/). NSF states the test as a review element: "Does the plan incorporate a mechanism to assess success?" ([NSF PAPPG, Chapter III](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-3-proposal-processing-review)).
- **Is the money right?** Carried by the [budget narrative](/encyclopedia/budgets/writing-a-budget-narrative/). The Office of Justice Programs states the standard plainly: "Your budget should justify all expenses and be consistent with the program narrative" ([OJP, Develop a Budget](https://www.ojp.gov/funding/grants101/develop-budget)).
- **Does the work survive the grant?** Carried by the [sustainability plan](/encyclopedia/proposal-craft/sustainability-plan/), which OJP frames as a request to "describe a plan for continuing your project beyond the grant period."

A section that proves nothing is padding, and padding is expensive because it consumes page limits that the scored sections need.

## How do grant proposal section names differ across funders?

Grant proposal section names differ substantially across funders while the underlying questions stay fixed. NIH organizes around Specific Aims, Significance, Innovation, Approach, Investigator, and Environment. NSF organizes around a Project Summary and Project Description. Federal program offices and foundations use plainer labels. Translating between vocabularies is a routine skill, not an advanced one.

The table below maps the six underlying questions to the section names used by federal program announcements, NIH, NSF, and most private foundations.

| Underlying question | Federal program announcement | NIH | NSF | Foundation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does the argument survive compression? | Project Abstract or Project Summary | Project Summary/Abstract; Specific Aims | Project Summary, with labeled Overview, Intellectual Merit, and Broader Impacts sections | Executive summary or cover letter |
| Is the problem real? | Statement of Need; Need for the Project | Significance | Motivation and background within the Project Description | Statement of Need; Problem Statement |
| What will change? | Goals and Objectives | Specific Aims | Objectives within the Project Description | Goals and Objectives |
| Can the activities produce the change? | Project Design; Approach; Work Plan | Approach; Research Strategy | Project Description | Program Description; Methods |
| Can the applicant execute? | Organizational Capacity; Management Plan | Investigator; Environment | Facilities, Equipment and Other Resources; Biographical Sketches | Organizational Background |
| Will success be measured? | Evaluation Plan; Performance Measures | Evaluation folded into Approach | Mechanism to assess success; Broader Impacts | Evaluation; Outcomes |

Two aliases cause the most confusion. **Specific Aims** at NIH is not a needs statement; it is a compressed statement of objectives and their rationale, "typically limited to one page and … often the only part of the proposal read by all reviewers, making it the most critical section of the entire application" ([Harvard Catalyst, Write Your Grant Proposal](https://catalyst.harvard.edu/writing-communication-center/get-funded/write-your-grant-proposal/)). **Project Narrative** means an attachment containing the full plan in most federal program announcements, but at NIH it means a short public-health-relevance statement. Read the announcement's own definition before assuming.

## Which grant proposal sections are scored and which are gates?

Scored grant proposal sections are the ones named in the review criteria; gates are compliance and responsiveness rules that end an application before any reviewer assigns a number. Gates are binary and usually unappealable, and they are stated in the announcement rather than hidden.

NSF publishes an explicit list of grounds for returning a proposal without review, including a proposal that "does not meet NSF proposal preparation requirements, such as page limitations, formatting instructions, and electronic submission," one that "is not responsive to the NSF funding opportunity," and one that "does not contain each of the required sections of the proposal" ([NSF PAPPG, Chapter IV.B](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-4-non-award-decisions-transactions)). The agency also requires literal headings: the Project Summary "must include separate sections labeled 'Overview,' 'Intellectual Merit,' and 'Broader Impacts'" and the Project Description "must include a section — with a heading — for 'Broader Impacts'" ([NSF Dear Colleague Letter 15-095](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/information/dcl-communication-about-automated-compliance-checking-proposal/nsf15-095)).

Purdue OWL states the general consequence bluntly: "many grantmakers will not read past the point of your departure from the application rules, no matter how worthy the project is," and "not following directions indicates carelessness—which is not a characteristic of a promising proposal" ([Purdue OWL, Grant Writing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/grant_writing/index.html)).

Three sections sit in an intermediate category. The abstract is frequently unscored yet determines which reviewers are assigned. The budget is often reviewed for allowability rather than scored. And attachments such as letters of commitment are checked for presence, not quality — until a missing one becomes a gate. A requirement-by-requirement compliance matrix is how applicants keep the two categories separate.

## How much space should each grant proposal section get?

Grant proposal page allocation should follow the published scoring weights, not the writer's interest. A criterion worth forty points and a criterion worth ten points should not receive equal pages, and no amount of writing quality corrects a misallocation set before drafting began.

Where a competition publishes no point weights, three signals substitute. First, the order and length of the instructions themselves — announcements spend words in rough proportion to what they care about. Second, the empirical pattern in expert review: at NIH, the Approach criterion tracks the final overall impact score more closely than Significance, Innovation, Investigator, or Environment ([NIA, The Approach criterion](https://www.nia.nih.gov/research/blog/2013/07/approach-criterion-why-does-it-matter-so-much-peer-review)). Third, the profession's own weighting of writing skill: the Grant Professional Certified writing exam scores persuasive argument far above mechanics, with formatting the smallest component of all; see the current figures above ([GPCI, Competencies Tested](https://www.grantcredential.org/the-exam/competencies-tested/)).

The abstract is the exception to proportional allocation. It is short by rule and disproportionate in effect. The University of Michigan's proposal guide puts it directly: "Some reviewers read only the abstract, e.g., a foundation board of directors' member who votes on final funding decisions. Thus it is the most important single element in the proposal." The same guide adds the sequencing rule — "Though it appears first, the abstract should be edited last" ([University of Michigan, Proposal Writer's Guide](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nextprof-science/wp-content/uploads/sites/130/2018/04/3-University-of-Michigan-Proposal-Writers-Guide.pdf)).

## What goes wrong with grant proposal structure?

Grant proposal structure fails in a small number of repeated ways, and each is visible in a draft before submission. Six are worth designing against:

- **Section names invented rather than borrowed.** Reviewers score against a rubric with specific headings; a narrative organized by the writer's logic forces them to hunt. NIH notes that "reviewers are accustomed to finding information in specific sections of the application" ([NIH, General Grant Writing Tips](https://grants.nih.gov/grants-process/write-application/general-grant-writing-tips)).
- **The need section describing the applicant.** A problem statement that requires the organization to exist is a want, not a need.
- **Objectives that restate the goal.** Direction substituted for destination leaves the evaluation section with nothing to measure.
- **A design that could not mechanically produce the outcomes.** Activities listed without dosage, sequence, or staffing assignment are a description of intent.
- **A budget that disagrees with the narrative.** A named coordinator with no salary line, or a travel line with no activity, reads as carelessness in the one section that is arithmetically checkable.
- **The abstract written last and treated as an afterthought.** Compression is where a weak argument becomes visible, which is exactly why it should be drafted last and revised most.

The compounding failure is treating the proposal as a document rather than as a set of proofs. A reviewer with limited time is looking for the specific sentence that discharges each burden, and a proposal that never states it plainly loses points it never contested.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the standard order of grant proposal sections?

Summary, need, goals and objectives, project design, capacity, evaluation, sustainability, budget. Funders vary the order, and the funder's order always wins. The University of Michigan's guide recommends using the sponsor's exact phrases as headings so reviewers find each element where the rubric tells them to look.

### Is the abstract part of the page limit?

Sometimes. Federal announcements state page limits in the application contents section, and agency-wide application guides frequently override them. NSF caps the Project Summary at one page separately from the Project Description. Treat every formatting rule as a responsiveness gate until the announcement says otherwise.

### What is the difference between a project narrative and a project summary?

A project narrative is the full plan submitted as an attachment in most federal competitions. A project summary is the compressed version, typically one page or less. At NIH the term "Project Narrative" instead means a brief public-health-relevance statement, which is a genuine source of confusion between agencies.

### Do foundation proposals use the same sections?

Yes, in compressed form. A foundation asking for a short narrative still expects need, plan, capacity, measurement, and budget. Many foundations gate access through a letter of inquiry first, which compresses the same six burdens into one or two pages before a full application is invited.

### Which grant proposal section carries the most weight?

Whichever the published criteria weight most heavily. In expert peer review at NIH, the Approach criterion correlates with the overall impact score more strongly than the other criteria. In point-weighted federal competitions, project design and need typically carry the largest shares.

### Can a proposal be rejected without being read?

Yes. NSF returns proposals without review for page limit violations, formatting violations, missing required sections, and non-responsiveness to the funding opportunity. Those outcomes produce no score and no substantive feedback, which is why compliance checking precedes drafting rather than following it.
